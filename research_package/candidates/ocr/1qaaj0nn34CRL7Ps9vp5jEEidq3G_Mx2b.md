Fabrication and Optical Properties of High-Performance Polycrystalline
Nd:YAG Ceramics for Solid-State Lasers

Akio Ikesue’ and Toshiyuki Kinoshita

Technical Research Center, Krosaki Corporation, Kitakyushu City 806, Japan

Kiichiro Kamata’

Department of Material Science and Technology, Nagaoka University of Technology, Nagaoka City 904-21, Japan

Transparent polycrystalline YAG with nearly the same
optical characteristics as those of a single crystal were fabri-
cated by a solid-state reaction method using high-purity
powders (>99.99 wt% purity). The average grain size and
relative density of the 1.1 at.% Nd:YAG ceramics obtained
were about 50 wm and 99.98%, respectively. An oscillation
experiment was performed on a cw laser by the diode laser
excitation system using the fabricated ceramics. The experi-
mental results indicated an oscillation threshold and a slope
efficiency of 309 mW and 28%, respectively. These values
were equivalent or superior to those of the 0.9 at.%
Nd:YAG single crystal fabricated by the Czochralski
method.

I. Introduction

See Maiman'” discovered the ruby laser in 1960, the solid-
state laser has been rapidly developed. In recent years, espe-
cially, solid-state lasers have been applied with remarkable
success in various industrial capacities such as for taking physi-
cal measurements and for medical uses. Although many kinds
of solid-state lasers exist, the YAG (Y,A1,O,,) single crystal
is the best laser host material. As a luminescent element, Nd
plays a more important role than do Ho, Tm, Er, etc. Because a
solid-state laser demands severe optical requirements, however,
synthesizing Nd:YAG laser material of polycrystalline ceramics
is technically very difficult.

Attempts to synthesize solid-state laser materials from poly-
crystalline ceramics such as Nd-doped Y,0,-ThO, ceramics
have been reported by Greskovich and Wood,’ and attempts
from YAG ceramics by de With and van Dijk,"° Mudler and de
With," and Sekita et al.'? In the experiments of both de With
and Sekita et al., polycrystalline YAG proved inadequate for
optical applications and unable to emit a laser beam.

The present work produced polycrystalline, transparent
Nd:YAG with optical characteristics nearly equal to those of a
single crystal. A simple, solid-state reaction among ALO,,
Y,0,, and Nd,O, powders (>99.99 wt%) was used in this
fabrication method. The Nd:YAG produced by that method
oscillated successfully by diode laser excitation.

In the present work, aggregated powders of Al,O,, Y,0;, and
Nd,O, with particles smaller than 2 j.m were used as starting
materials. We had synthesized the Al,O;, Y,0;, and Nd,O,
powders by alkoxide precipitation, pyrolysis of Y,(OH)CI,-
nH,O, and oxalate precipitation, respectively. The impurity
content of each powder was confirmed by ICP to be less than
100 wt ppm. The primary particles of the starting powders were
observed by TEM (in the case of Y,0,) and SEM (A1,O, and
Nd,O,). To fabricate the specimens, these starting powders
were weighed so that the Nd content in the resultant YAG
ceramics would be 1.1 at.%.

After the Al,O,, Y,0;, and Nd,O, powders had been mixed
with ethyl silicate as a sintering aid (0.5 wt% in ethyl alcohol),
the mixture was milled for 12 h with high-purity A1,O, balls
(>99.9 wt%). The milled slurry was dried using a spray dryer.
The granulated, spherical powders, measuring less than 150 pm
in diameter, were isostatically pressed at 140 MPa into disks
16 mm in diameter. To reveal sintering shrinkage behavior, the
powder compact was heated by TMA at 5°C/min from room
temperature to 1650°C in an oxygen atmosphere (c-axis sap-
phire was used as a reference). The powder compact then was
heated at 5°C/min and cooled down at 20°C/min from the
appropriate temperatures (no soaking time) to enable observa-
tion of microstructural changes. The constituent phases in each
specimen were identified by X-ray powder diffractometry.

Specimens for optical measurement were sintered at 1750°C
for 1 to 50 h under vacuum at 1.3 X 10° Pa pressure. The
microstructure after sintering was observed by reflection and
polarized light using an optical microscope as well as SEM,
EPMA, and TEM.

Disk specimens 10 mm in diameter to be used for measuring
absorption and fluorescence spectra were polished on both sur-
faces below the average microroughness (R,) of 3 nm. The
absorption spectrum of each specimen was measured over the
wavelength region 400 to 1000 nm with a 0.5-nm slit width and
a 120-nm/min scan speed, using a spectrophotometer (U-3500,
Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). For measuring the fluorescence spec-
trum, the specimen was excited with an 820-nm diode laser
(output power, 15 mW), and the fluorescence was recorded
around a 1-~m region using an Si photodiode detector. A
0.9 at.% Nd:YAG single crystal (Tokin, Japan) was used as a
reference for each measurement.

Tl. Results

Figure 2 shows TEM and SEM photographs of the Y,O,,
Al,O,, and Nd,O, powders used as starting materials. The pri-
mary particle diameters of the Y,0;, Al,O;, and Nd,O, were
60, 400, and 500 nm, respectively.

Figure 3 shows the sintering shrinkage behavior of the pow-
der compacts in the system Al,O,-Y,0, without Nd,O,.
Remarkable shrinkage began near 1100°C, and the shrinkage
rate then decreased around 1300° to 1500°C. Shrinkage
increased again above 1500°C, as shown in Fig. 3. The dashed
line in Fig. 3 indicates the shrinkage behavior of another pow-
der compact composed of commercial Y,O, and Al,O, powders
which are synthesized by oxalate precipitation (Mitsubishi
Kasei, 40 nm in diameter) and ammonium alum precipitation
(Taimei Kagaku, 300 nm). These powder compacts were treated
by the same procedure and pressed into the powder compacts.
Shrinkage of the commercial powder compact began at a
slightly lower temperature than that recorded in the present
work, but this powder compact expanded near 1450°C, and
only a slight shrinkage with densificaton took place at higher
temperatures. As shown in Fig. 3, it is difficult to obtain fully
dense polycrystal YAG using commercial powders.

Figure 4 shows XRD patterns of the specimen heated at
1100°, 1300°, 1500°, and 1700°C. Figure 5 illustrates the phases
produced in the specimen, as measured every 100°C by XRD.
The net width on the ordinate represents the qualitative amount

dense, nearly pore-free structure was observed at 1700°C.

Figure 7 shows the relative density of powder compacts

density of the specimen sintered at 1600°C was 98.2%, and the
value approached a theoretical amount at 1700°C or above.

Figure 8 shows a reflection microscope photograph, after
thermal etching, of 1.1 at.% Nd:YAG sintered at 1750°C for
20 h under vacuum. The specimen consisted of uniform grains
measuring about 50 um.

Figure 9 shows polarizing microscope (transmission micro-
scope) photographs of the same specimen, under both open and
cross nicols. A nearly pore-free structure was observed in the
specimen in the depth direction by open nicol, and perfect
optical isotropy also was confirmed in the dark-field image
under cross nicols.

Figure 14 shows the absorption spectra (specimen thick-

Figure 15 shows the relationship between the logarithm of

laser oscillation area and exhibited no optical absorption by the
Nd element. Almost no optical attenuation was observed with
increasing specimen thickness for the single-crystal or the poly-
crystal specimens. The optical attenuation coefficient of the
Nd:YAG polycrystal, as obtained from the gradient of the
Fig. 15 line, was about 1.1 X 10-? cm™! (0.9% cm™'), which is
almost the same as that for a single crystal.

Figure 16 shows the fluorescence spectra of 1.1 at.%
Nd:YAG polycrystal and of a 0.9 at.% Nd:YAG single crystal
excited by a diode laser of 820 nm. Profiles of the fluorescence
spectra of the Nd:YAG polycrystal and the Nd:YAG single
crystal are consistent with each other, although the peak intensi-
ties of the polycrystal are larger than those of the single crystal
because of differences in Nd content.

Figure 17 shows photographs of 1.1 at.% Nd:YAG polycrys-
tal (8 mm diameter by 2 mm) observed by both direct and
polarizing light. Usually, an Nd:YAG single crystal formed by
the Czochralski (CZ) technique forms the core (optical strain
part) at the center of the ingot, but the Nd:YAG polycrystal
obtained in the present work had a core-free body (optically
homogeneous), as shown in the photograph.

highly dense YAG by a solid-state reaction and vacuum sinter-
ing is very difficult, as shown in the commercial powder of
Fig. 3, because a YAG (4.55 g/cm’) single phase is produced
via a higher-density intermediate phase of YAIO, (5.35 g/cm’).
In the YAG system, there have been no reports of the fabrication
of highly dense ceramics (translucent ceramics) by only a solid-
state reaction and vacuum sintering. Apparently, the most
important factor in fabricating highly dense YAG ceramics is
the use of AI,O, and Y,O, powders with high reactivity as
well as different particle sizes. The selection of an appropriate
starting material needs further explanation regarding its impor-
tance to the fabrication of transparent YAG polycrystal by a
solid-state reaction, and a detailed discussion on densification
is presented elsewhere."

Optical characteristics such as transmittance, fluorescence
lifetime, and fluorescence spectra were similar to those of an
Nd:YAG single crystal. For a solid-state laser, the beam must
be amplified at the inside of the materials, and hence a high-
quality fabricating technique is essential to assure that scatter-
ing loss is nearly zero. It would be quite difficult to fabricate
transparent polycrystals with a very low scattering loss by con-
ventional processing techniques. The transparent polycrystals
prepared up to now by HP and HIP using coprecipitated pow-
ders from aqueous solutions have not been of sufficient optical
quality in comparison with that of single crystals.'*"’ The opti-
cal attenuation of YAG polycrystals synthesized by de With and
van Dijk,'° Mudler and de With," and Sekita et al.'* was too
great to allow their use in solid-state lasers. No work has yet
achieved efficient oscillation using YAG polycrystal, because
scattering loss far exceeds amplification by the luminescent
elements in ceramic laser materials. Conventionally, the optical
properties of transparent polycrystal have been reported only
for very thin specimens (around 1 mm thick) because of their
lower optical quality: the transmittance of the thin specimens
easily surpasses that of the bulky samples. Consequently, the
thickness of specimens must be taken into account.

The optical attenuation (transmittance) of ceramics is
affected mainly by pores, inclusions, and grain boundaries, so
that these effects increase logarithmically with the increasing
thickness of the specimens, according to Lambert—Beer’s law.

A linear relationship is observed between logarithmic transmit-
tance and specimen thickness, as shown in Fig. 15. This figure
suggests that the YAG polycrystals obtained in the present
work have extremely low optical attenuation loss. The optical
attenuation coefficient (B) can be obtained from the gradient in
Fig. 15. The B value can be composed of the linear absorption
coefficient (a) and the scattering coefficient (S) caused in the
specimen by the scattering medium. Both the Nd:YAG poly-
crystal and the Nd:YAG single crystal showed little optical

absorption by luminescent elements at the measured wave-
lengths. The a value therefore is speculated to be fairly small.
The scattering loss of the Nd:YAG polycrystal can be estimated
at about 0.9% cm™', because optical attenuation supposedly
depends only on the amount of scattering substance (8 = S,
S>> a).

The Nd:YAG polycrystals fabricated in the present study
were able to produce an oscillating laser beam, as mentioned
earlier. From the viewpoint of microstructure, this phenomenon
can be explained as follows. Structural differences between the
single crystal and the polycrystal result in (1) the existence of a
grain boundary and (2) absolute pore volume. If scattering
is affected mainly by the grain boundary of polycrystalline
ceramics, then scattering must be repeated continuously with
every exciting beam and fluorescence beam (laser beam) that
passes through the inside of the specimen. Such beams could be
considered to cross the grain boundary anywhere from several
hundred to several thousand times. If the grain boundary were
the main factor for scattering, therefore, part of the beam pro-
ceeding through the ceramics would leak out. The total amount
of beam leaking from the exciting and laser beams, which cross
thousands of grain boundaries, thus would become enormous,
as these beams reciprocated through the optical resonator. In
the present case, it would be difficult for Nd: YAG polycrystal
to oscillate as strongly as does an Nd:YAG single crystal. The
TEM photograph shows a perfect structure expanding up to the
vicinity of the grain boundary. The optical anisotropic phase
also was confirmed by polarizing microscopy to be free around
the grain boundary. The optical properties are macroscopically
perfect because the entire specimen is observed through a polar-
izing plate. It has been shown that the main factor influencing
beam scattering is not considered to be the grain boundary, but
